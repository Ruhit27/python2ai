---
description: Spreading the cost of a long-lasting asset across the years it's used, rather than counting it all at once.
---

The way accounting spreads the cost of a long-lasting [asset](./Asset.md) over the years it will be used, instead of treating the whole price as a cost in the year it was bought.

If a coffee shop buys a $10,000 espresso machine expected to last five years, it might record $2,000 of depreciation each year. The [income statement](./Income%20statement.md) shows a $2,000 [cost](./Cost.md) each year, and the machine's value on the [balance sheet](./Balance%20sheet.md) drops by $2,000 a year until it reaches zero. This matches the cost to the years the machine actually helps earn [revenue](./Revenue.md).

Depreciation doesn't involve any cash leaving the business — the cash left when the machine was bought. That's why measures like [EBITDA](./EBITDA.md) add it back. The same idea applied to non-physical assets like software or patents is called *amortization*.

_Avoid:_ "loss of value" in the everyday sense — accounting depreciation follows a set schedule, not the machine's real resale price.

_Usage:_

"Why does the report show a cost for the servers when we paid for them last year?"

"That's depreciation — we're spreading last year's purchase across the next four years."

---
description: Profit shown as a percentage of revenue. Also the gap between price and cost on a single sale.
---

Profit expressed as a share of [revenue](./Revenue.md), so businesses of different sizes can be compared. If a coffee shop earns $4,000 and keeps $600 as [profit](./Profit.md), its margin is 15%.

The word has two closely related meanings. As a **percentage**, margin is profit ÷ revenue: gross margin uses gross profit, net margin uses net profit. On a **single sale**, margin is the gap between the price and the cost of that item: a coffee that sells for $4 and costs $1 to make has a $3 margin, or 75%. Both answer the same question — how much of each dollar in do you keep? — one for a single item, the other for the whole business.

Margins differ hugely by industry. Software can have gross margins above 80% because copying code costs almost nothing; supermarkets often run net margins of 1–3% and survive on volume. So a "good" margin only means something against similar businesses.

_Avoid:_ "markup" is not the same: markup divides profit by cost, margin divides it by price. A $1 item sold for $4 has a 300% markup but a 75% margin.

_Usage:_

"Should we cut the price to win the deal?"

"Our margin on that plan is already thin — a 20% discount would make every sale a loss."

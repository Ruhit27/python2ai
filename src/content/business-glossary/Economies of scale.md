---
description: When making more of something lowers the cost of each unit. Bigger can mean cheaper.
---

The cost advantage that comes from producing more: as volume goes up, the average [cost](./Cost.md) of each unit goes down. This happens because fixed costs are spread over more units, and because big buyers get better deals.

A bakery that pays $3,000 a month for its oven and rent spends $3 of that per loaf if it bakes 1,000 loaves, but only $0.30 per loaf at 10,000. A supermarket chain buying millions of cans can negotiate lower prices from suppliers than a corner shop can. Software shows extreme economies of scale: building it is expensive, but serving one more user costs almost nothing.

Economies of scale are a major source of [competitive advantage](./Competitive%20advantage.md): the biggest player can charge less, or keep a higher [margin](./Margin.md) at the same price. They can reverse, though — past a certain size, complexity, management layers, and coordination costs can make a company slower and more expensive (*diseconomies of scale*). They are part of what makes a big [market share](./Market%20share.md) valuable.

_Avoid:_ "growth" — growing is not the same as getting cheaper per unit; economies of scale is the second thing.

_Usage:_

"How can they sell it cheaper than it costs us to make?"

"Economies of scale. They produce 50 times our volume and pay far less per part."

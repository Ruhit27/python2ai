---
description: Return on investment: how much you gain from spending money on something, compared to what it cost.
---

Stands for **R**eturn **O**n **I**nvestment: a measure of how much an investment earns compared to what it cost. The basic formula is (gain − cost) ÷ cost. Spend $10,000 on a machine that saves $15,000 over its life, and the ROI is 50%.

ROI makes very different options comparable: a marketing campaign, a new hire, new software, a new store. It is commonly used to justify spending in a [budget](./Budget.md) and to judge afterwards whether it paid off. Related comparisons, like [LTV](./LTV.md) against [CAC](./CAC.md), are really ROI calculations for winning customers.

Its weaknesses: ROI ignores time (50% over one year is far better than 50% over ten), is only as good as the estimated gains, and doesn't show what else the money could have done — that's [opportunity cost](./Opportunity%20cost.md). And past spending that can't be recovered shouldn't count in the calculation at all; see [sunk cost](./Sunk%20cost.md).

_Avoid:_ "profit" — ROI is a ratio of gain to cost, not an amount of money.

_Usage:_

"The new CRM costs $30k a year. Is it worth it?"

"If it saves the sales team 10 hours a week each, the ROI is well over 100%. Let's check that estimate."

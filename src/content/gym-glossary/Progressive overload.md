---
description: Gradually making training harder over time — more weight, reps, or sets — so the body keeps having a reason to adapt.
---

The core principle of getting stronger and building muscle: gradually increasing the demand on the body over time. Muscles adapt to what they're asked to do; once a workout becomes easy, it stops being a reason to change.

The most common way is to add weight. But overload can also mean more [reps](./Rep.md) with the same weight, more [sets](./Set.md) ([volume](./Volume.md)), a fuller [range of motion](./Range%20of%20motion.md), or better [form](./Form.md) at the same load. A beginner might add 2.5 kg (5 lb) to their squat every session for months; an experienced lifter might only add that much in a month.

When progress stops for weeks despite effort, that's a [plateau](./Plateau.md), and the usual fixes are adjusting the [program](./Program.md), taking a [deload](./Deload.md), or sorting out sleep and food. Progressive overload is why a written program — or at least a training log — is so useful: you can't beat last week if you don't know what you did.

_Avoid:_ "muscle confusion" — constantly changing exercises isn't needed; steady, measurable progress on the same lifts works better.

_Usage:_

"I've done the same workout for six months and look the same."

"You're lifting the same weights too. You need progressive overload — add a rep or a little weight each week."

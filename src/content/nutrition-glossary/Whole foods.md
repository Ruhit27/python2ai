Foods that are eaten in or close to their natural state, with little processing: vegetables, fruit, eggs, fish, meat, beans, lentils, nuts, whole grains, plain dairy. (Not to be confused with the supermarket brand.)

Diets built mostly on whole foods tend to be higher in [fiber](./Fiber.md), [micronutrients](./Micronutrient.md), and water, and lower in [added sugar](./Added%20sugar.md) and [sodium](./Sodium.md). They also tend to be more filling ([satiety](./Satiety.md)) and have lower [calorie density](./Calorie%20density.md), which makes controlling calories easier without counting.

The opposite end of the spectrum is [ultra-processed food](./Ultra-processed%20food.md). Some processing is fine or helpful — frozen vegetables, canned beans, and plain yogurt are processed but still nutritious. A practical goal is to make most of what you eat whole or minimally processed, not to avoid all processed food.

_Avoid:_ "clean eating" — it sounds precise but has no clear definition, and can make normal foods feel "dirty".

_Usage:_

"What's the easiest diet change I can make?"

"Base most meals on whole foods — you'll likely eat fewer calories without trying."
